The Quiet Joy of Reading Slowly

By Jane Doe · March 4, 2025

Share on Facebook Post

There is a particular pleasure in reading slowly, one that the modern internet seems designed to erase. When we skim, we gather facts; when we linger, we gather meaning. This essay is about the difference between those two activities and why it matters more than ever, now that almost everything we read arrives on a glowing rectangle that also wants to sell us shoes.

Consider the humble e-reader. Unlike a phone, it offers no notifications, no infinite scroll, and no autoplaying videos. Its screen refreshes with a faint flash, like a page turning in a quiet room. People who switch to one often report that they read more, and that they remember more of what they read, even though the device can do far less than the phone in their pocket.

Part of the reason is friction. A book, paper or electronic, ends. It has a last page, and reaching it feels like an accomplishment rather than an interruption. The feed never ends, so there is never a natural moment to stop and think about what we have just taken in. We simply keep going, hoping the next item will be the one that satisfies.

Researchers who study attention describe two modes of reading. In the first, the eyes jump across the page looking for keywords, and comprehension is shallow but fast. In the second, the reader follows the argument sentence by sentence, holding earlier points in mind while weighing new ones. The second mode is slower and more tiring, but it is the only one in which a text can change your mind.

We read to know we are not alone, and we read slowly to know that we have understood.

None of this means that skimming is bad. There are many things worth skimming: timetables, weather forecasts, most email. The trouble starts when skimming becomes the only way we read, when even long and careful pieces of writing get the same hurried glance as a list of train times. The writer spent weeks on the essay; the reader spends ninety seconds.

So here is a modest proposal. Once a day, gather a few things worth reading, put them somewhere without links to follow or buttons to press, and read them the way you would read a letter from a friend: from beginning to end, with your full attention, and without checking what else has arrived in the meantime.
